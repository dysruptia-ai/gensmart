To know whether your customer service works, look at four indicators: response time, conversations handed to a person, unanswered questions and appointments booked. With them you see whether people write to you, whether they get a timely reply, what your knowledge base is missing and whether conversations end in something concrete. You do not need outside benchmarks: comparing yourself with yourself week after week is what helps.

## What does response time tell you?

It tells you how long a person waits from the moment they write until they get something useful. Measure it by time slot: day, night and weekends behave differently. If it is short by day and long at night, that is the gap to close. Count the first useful reply, not an automatic greeting.

## What do handed-off conversations tell you?

They tell you how much the agent resolves on its own and what falls to your team. A high number is not bad in itself: you may have many complex sales. What matters is the reason. If they pass because information is missing, you fix it by expanding the base; if they pass because the case needs judgment, that is expected.

## Why do unanswered questions matter?

Because they are the to-do list for your knowledge base. Each question the agent could not answer is an answer you can write once that serves all later conversations. It is the indicator with the most direct improvement.

## What do booked appointments tell you?

They tell you whether service ends in a concrete step. Compare how many high-interest conversations ended in an appointment and review those that did not: sometimes the time offered did not work or the person never received a proposal. If your business does not work with appointments, swap this indicator for your final action, whether that is a quote sent or a visit.

One last piece of advice: do not chase a perfect number. An indicator is for deciding what to fix this week, not for bragging. If a number gets worse, look for the cause in the specific conversations before changing anything.

:::nota
Pick a fixed day to review the four indicators and note what you changed that week. That way you will know which adjustment improved what.
:::

A good starting point is to measure a normal week without changing anything. That first reading is your baseline: it is neither good nor bad, it is yours, and everything you adjust afterward is compared against it.

## How do you read them together?

- Slow replies with many unanswered questions: information is missing from the base.
- Many handed-off conversations and few appointments: review the handoff rule and the calendar.
- Few conversations overall: the problem is earlier, in how people find you.
